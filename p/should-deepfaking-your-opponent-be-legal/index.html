Should deepfaking your opponent be legal?

Two campaigns posted AI videos of their opponents saying things they never said, and when one got a cease-and-desist, he answered with an AI duck.

The Take By VPRWRE Oct. 4, 2026, 8:30 a.m. PT

In Texas, Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick’s campaign posted AI videos of challenger Vikki Goodwin. On Sept 19 she filed a criminal complaint. The posts came down the next day, the Texas Tribune reported. His spokesman, Allen Blakemore, calls them “an obvious parody produced to entertain.”

In Wisconsin, Rep. Derrick Van Orden posted AI videos of Rebecca Cooke, made with Grok, WPR reports. “Putting words in my mouth,” Cooke said. Her lawyers sent a cease-and-desist. He replied with an AI duck yelling “Cease and desist!” His team calls the videos “obviously AI-generated satire.”

The Wesleyan Media Project counted 164 AI-made or AI-enhanced political ads this cycle; 69% had no disclosure, AP reported. About 31 states regulate political deepfakes, per NCSL, with gaps: Wisconsin’s law covers paid ads, not free posts. In 2024, the FEC declined to write AI-specific rules.

Our take: a fake candidate isn’t satire. Satire needs a label. A real candidate’s face saying invented lines, posted as ballots go out, is a forgery.

On the record Oct 3, 2026

By Jan 1, 2028, no federal law regulating AI deepfakes in campaign ads has been signed.

If anyone can be faked, nobody can be believed.
