Did AI write France’s No. 1 novel?

A detector said yes. Then France’s top literary prize dropped him.

The Take By VPRWRE Oct. 3, 2026, 4:27 a.m. PT

On Sept 21, Thélyson Orélien’s debut novel, C’était ça ou mourir, won the Prix du roman Fnac. That night, an X account called “Balance ton Claude,” with 92 followers, posted detector scores calling it “almost entirely” AI. The post passed 3 million views.

Orélien denied it: “I don’t see why I’d turn to a machine.” His publisher, Grasset, called it a hate campaign. Goncourt president Philippe Claudel asked “why now?” and pointed out the author is Black, Haitian and Canadian.

Then reporters found real plagiarism in pieces he wrote in 2012–2014. On Sept 25, the Académie Goncourt pulled the novel from its list, citing plagiarism and analyses saying it is “in all likelihood, very largely” AI. Columnist Samuel Fitoussi has confirmed he was behind the account.

The machines don’t agree. Pangram says 92–100% AI. Another tool AFP ran called it “very likely” human. The drafts Orélien promised still haven’t been shown.

Readers kept buying: 65,000 copies, and France’s No. 1 novel the week it was dropped.

Software flagged it. A jury removed it. Readers bought it.

So who gets to decide what’s human?

Background

The Académie Goncourt awards one of France’s most prestigious literary prizes; it plans to publish its second selection on Oct. 6 and name a winner on Nov. 4. Before the dispute, Orélien’s novel had also won the Prix Méduse and the Prix Première Plume and was on early lists for the Renaudot, Femina and Médicis prizes, according to Livres Hebdo. Orélien, 38, is a Canadian-Haitian writer. His debut follows a Haitian migrant’s journey through Latin America to Canada.

AI detectors such as Pangram, the tool the account cited, give estimates of how likely a text is to be machine-written. They do not give definitive verdicts, as the French tech site Presse-Citron noted. Results in this case have split. AFP ran the novel through eight detectors: two rated it very probably AI-generated, three reached the opposite conclusion and three gave intermediate scores of 7 to 59 percent, NR Magazine reported. A 2023 Stanford study found that detectors flagged 61 percent of essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated, though it did not test Pangram.

His Canadian publisher, Boréal, suspended promotion on Sept. 24 and said it could not confirm the accusations. Grasset, his French publisher, has stood by him. Orélien says the book’s imagery, repetition and rhythm come from Haitian and Caribbean language traditions, The Next Web reported. He has called the allegations racially motivated but has not addressed the plagiarism findings.
