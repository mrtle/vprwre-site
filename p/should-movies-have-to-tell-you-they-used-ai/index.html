Should movies have to tell you they used AI?

Danny Boyle did.

The Take By VPRWRE Oct. 3, 2026, 4:30 p.m. PT

On Sept 5, Boyle told Vanity Fair his newspaper drama Ink has “about 30 seconds” of AI: a couple of animated old photographs. “Oh, and there’s some mice.” At its TIFF Q&A on Sept 15, an audience member reportedly shouted “Never do that sh*t again.” The Q&A ended soon after.

Ben Affleck took the other route. On Sept 30, at Bloomberg’s Screentime, he said he used AI “for lots of things” in Animals, his Netflix film out Oct 9, and won’t say where. His reason: a magician who announces “there’s another bunny under here” ruins the show. Netflix paid $587M for his AI startup, InterPositive, and says about 300 of its titles already use generative AI.

So the fight isn’t over how much AI. It’s who gets told. Actors won consent: Oscar performances must now come from humans “with their consent.” Audiences got nothing.

Our take: label it anyway. Disclosure won’t stop every heckle. But when the audience finds out on its own, the whole film becomes the trick.

On the record Oct 2, 2026

By the 100th Academy Awards, the Academy requires every film submitted to disclose its generative AI use.

Magic works because you know it’s a trick.

Would you watch a film differently if you knew where the AI was?
